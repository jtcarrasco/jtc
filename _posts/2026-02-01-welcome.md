I've been building websites for a living for over 15 years. For most of that time, "web developer" meant custom codebases, client projects, turning designs into real pages, and the kind of maintenance work that keeps the internet running without anybody noticing. At [Edlio](https://www.edlio.com) I worked on more than 350 school websites across a whole mix of languages and stacks. Outside of that, I've built and looked after WordPress sites for clients ranging from small businesses to big organizations. They all wanted the same thing, really: make it work, and then make it so they never have to think about it again.

That's the job. It's real work, and I take it seriously.

What's changed lately is everything around the job. Over the last year or so I've been building a layer of AI-powered stuff on top of my day-to-day: automated agents, self-hosted tools and a homelab I actually use. A job search agent that runs while I sleep. A wiki that reads articles and links them together on its own. A daily briefing that hits my Discord before I've had my coffee.

This blog is where I write about what I built, why I built it and what I'd do differently next time.

## The professional bit

I work right where design meets development. I know that sounds like professional spiel, but it's a real and useful skill. A lot of projects fall apart in the gap between "here's the mockup" and "here's the live site," and I close that gap. I can look at a design, understand what it's trying to do, and build it without needing a second person to translate.

Most of my client work is in WordPress, though I've written enough React, PHP and custom JavaScript to have opinions about all three. Strong ones.

## The automation bit

Over the past few years I've gotten more and more interested in the places where AI and automation can take repetitive brain work off my plate entirely. Not in the "AI will replace developers" sense, which honestly bores me as a conversation. More in the "I can describe what I want in a markdown file and an agent goes and does it" sense, which I find really useful. I want to be the one steering the thing, not the one being steered by it.

## Why a blog

Mostly to have somewhere to put the things I've figured out. The posts skip the setup instructions and get to the decisions. If you want the nuts and bolts of how I built something, look for the posts tagged [tutorial](/tags/?tag=tutorial).
